Six labels, three hundred unlabeled points. Let the shape of the data do the rest.

Labels are expensive. Transcribing an hour of speech takes many hours, and a medical image may need a doctor. Unlabeled inputs are cheap. Semi-supervised learning (§19.3) uses lots of unlabeled \(\mathbf x\sim p(\mathbf x)\) to learn the overall shape of the data, and only a few labeled pairs \((\mathbf x,y)\sim p(\mathbf x,y)\) to say which part is which class.

This lab covers five ways to do that. In self-training the model labels the data for itself (19.3.1). Entropy minimization pushes the model toward confident predictions, which moves its boundary into the gaps between clusters (19.3.2). In co-training and tri-training, two or three models label data for each other (19.3.3). Label propagation lets labels flow along a similarity graph (19.3.4). Consistency regularization asks for the same prediction when the input is perturbed (19.3.5). Every model on the page is trained live in your browser.

§19.3

Semi-supervised learning: labels are scarce, inputs are not

Supervised learning assumes a labeled sample from the joint distribution, \(\mathcal D_L=\{(\mathbf x_n,y_n)\}_{n=1}^{N_L}\sim p(\mathbf x,y)\). Semi-supervised learning adds an unlabeled sample from the marginal, \(\mathcal D_U=\{\mathbf x_m\}_{m=1}^{N_U}\sim p(\mathbf x)\), usually with \(N_U\gg N_L\) (Fig. 19.8). The aim is to learn the high-level structure of the data from \(\mathcal D_U\) and use \(\mathcal D_L\) only for the fine details.

Unlabeled points carry no \(y\). They help only if we assume something that links \(p(\mathbf x)\) to \(p(y\mid\mathbf x)\). Every method in this section makes such an assumption: that classes form clusters, or that nearby points share a label.

Explain itAt your level

Imagine sorting a huge pile of seashells, but your teacher only tells you the names of six of them.

Six shells isn't much to learn from. But when you spread all the shells out on the floor, you notice they fall into two big piles: long curly ones over here and flat round ones over there. Nobody told you that. You saw it.

Now the six named shells just tell you which pile is which. Three of them sit in the curly pile and are called "whelks," so the whole curly pile is probably whelks.

That's semi-supervised learning. The shells without names show you the piles, and the few named ones tell you what each pile is called. Names are hard to get (someone has to know them) but shells are everywhere.

Why not just label everything? Because labels cost time and money. Writing down every word in an hour of recorded speech takes many hours. Labeling a tumor on a scan needs a doctor. Recording people talking, or collecting scans, is cheap by comparison.

So we usually have a few labeled examples (input and answer) and a lot of unlabeled ones (input only). The unlabeled inputs can't tell us the answers, but they show us where the data lives: the clumps, curves and gaps.

If we assume that points in the same clump share a label, a couple of labels per clump are enough. In the Play box, start with 1 or 2 labels per class. A normal classifier trained on just those points usually cuts right through the moon shapes. Turn on the unlabeled points and the right answer is obvious to your eye. Label propagation, a method from §19.3.4, uses those points and gets it almost exactly right.

Setup: \(\mathcal D_L\sim p(\mathbf x,y)\), \(\mathcal D_U\sim p(\mathbf x)\), \(N_U\gg N_L\), and we want \(p(y\mid\mathbf x)\). The unlabeled data can only matter through an assumption that ties \(p(\mathbf x)\) to \(p(y\mid\mathbf x)\):

  • Cluster assumption (§19.3.2.1): classes form clusters, so the decision boundary should fall in a low-density region of \(p(\mathbf x)\).
  • Manifold assumption (§19.3.4): points that are "similar" (close along the data manifold) probably share a label.

Most methods here can be written as a supervised loss plus an unlabeled term, \(\sum_{(\mathbf x,y)\in\mathcal D_L}\ell(y,f(\mathbf x))+\lambda\sum_{\mathbf x\in\mathcal D_U}r(f,\mathbf x)\). For self-training, \(r\) is the loss against the model's own hard prediction. For entropy minimization, \(r\) is the entropy of \(p_\theta(y\mid\mathbf x)\). Label propagation instead solves for labels on a graph directly.

  • Why an assumption is required. Take a discriminative model \(p(y\mid\mathbf x,\theta)\) and a separate input model \(p(\mathbf x\mid\mu)\), with \(\theta\) and \(\mu\) independent a priori. Then the posterior over \(\theta\) doesn't depend on \(\mathcal D_U\) at all (Seeger's argument). Every SSL method breaks this independence somehow: low-density separation, graph or manifold regularization, generative models \(p(\mathbf x\mid y)\), or consistency under augmentation.
  • When it hurts. If the assumption is wrong (classes overlap in dense regions, or the unlabeled data have a different class mix or extra classes), unlabeled data can make things worse. Oliver et al. (2018), "Realistic evaluation of deep SSL algorithms", show that gains shrink under class-distribution mismatch and with a properly tuned supervised baseline.
  • Transductive vs inductive. Some methods only label the given \(\mathcal D_U\) (label propagation). Others produce \(f\) for any new \(\mathbf x\). You can always turn the first into the second by training a supervised model on the inferred labels.
  • Where this goes next. The rest of §19.3 in the book (consistency regularization, MixMatch/FixMatch, generative SSL) builds on these four ideas. FixMatch, for example, is online pseudo-labeling with a confidence threshold plus augmentation.

Two samples, one target

  1. Labeled: \(\mathcal D_L=\{(\mathbf x_n,y_n)\}_{n=1}^{N_L}\), drawn from the joint \(p(\mathbf x,y)\). This is ordinary supervised data.
  2. Unlabeled: \(\mathcal D_U=\{\mathbf x_m\}_{m=1}^{N_U}\), drawn from the marginal \(p(\mathbf x)=\sum_yp(\mathbf x,y)\), with \(N_U\gg N_L\). Fig. 19.8: same inputs, labels hidden.

§19.3.1

Self-training and pseudo-labeling: grade your own homework

Train on the labeled data. Predict the unlabeled data. Treat the predictions as labels (pseudo-labels) and train again. Any model that can make predictions can do this, which is why the method has lasted since the 1960s [Scu65].

The danger is confirmation bias [TV17]. A wrong prediction becomes a training label, the model learns it, and the error locks in. The usual defense is a selection metric: only keep a pseudo-label when the model's top class probability is above a threshold \(\tau\).

Explain itAt your level

A student does a worksheet with no answer key, grades it with their own best guesses, then studies from it.

If the student already knows a little, this helps. They're right about the easy questions, and practicing those makes them better at the medium ones. Next time they can grade a few more.

But if they started with a wrong idea, like "spiders are insects," they mark that answer right and practice it again and again. Now they believe it even more. That's confirmation bias: you keep "confirming" your own mistake.

A good rule: only grade the questions you're really sure about. Skip the rest until you've learned more.

The recipe:

  1. Train a model on the few labeled points.
  2. Ask it about every unlabeled point. If it says "class B, 97% sure," write down "B" as a pseudo-label.
  3. Keep only the pseudo-labels above a confidence threshold \(\tau\), say 95%.
  4. Retrain on the real labels plus the pseudo-labels. Go back to step 2.

The threshold is a trade-off. Low \(\tau\) (like 50%) labels everything at once, mistakes included, and the model just re-learns its first guess. High \(\tau\) adds only the safest points, so the labels spread out slowly from the regions it's sure about. In the Play box, compare τ = 0.5 with τ = 0.95 on Draw A, then try Draw B, where a bad start goes wrong.

Two procedures, both common:

  • Offline: pseudo-label all of \(\mathcal D_U\), retrain (maybe from scratch) to convergence on \(\mathcal D_L\cup\) pseudo-labels, re-label, repeat. Works well with huge unlabeled sets [Yal+19; Xie+20]. Downside: labels go stale between re-labelings. The Play box does this.
  • Online: each step, take a random batch of unlabeled points, pseudo-label them with the current model, and take a gradient step on them right away. Labels are always fresh, but you pay for constant re-labeling. Used inside methods like FixMatch [Soh+20].

Neither is fundamentally better. Selection metric: keep \(\hat y=\arg\max_cp_\theta(y=c\mid\mathbf x)\) only if \(\max_cp_\theta(y=c\mid\mathbf x)\ge\tau\). If the probabilities are well calibrated, a kept label is correct with probability at least \(\tau\), as judged by the model.

  • Hard EM view. Treat the unknown labels as latent variables. Offline self-training alternates an E-step that takes the argmax label (classification EM) with an M-step that refits. Like hard EM, it can converge to a fixed point that just reproduces the initial model. That is what τ = 0.5 does in the demo.
  • Calibration is the weak link. Deep nets are often overconfident (Guo et al. 2017), so "p ≥ 0.95" can be wrong far more than 5% of the time. Practical fixes include class-balanced selection, per-class or curriculum thresholds, and temperature scaling before thresholding.
  • Modern versions. Noisy Student (Xie et al. 2020) is offline: a teacher labels 300M JFT images, and a larger student trained with dropout, stochastic depth and RandAugment becomes the next teacher. FixMatch (Sohn et al. 2020) is online: pseudo-label a weakly augmented view with τ = 0.95 and train on a strongly augmented view. The noise and augmentation keep the student from simply copying the teacher.
  • Theory. Wei et al. (2021) show self-training can provably fix pseudo-label errors under an "expansion" assumption on the class-conditional distributions, plus a consistency regularizer. This is a formal version of the cluster assumption.

One round of offline self-training

  1. Fit \(\theta_0=\arg\min_\theta\sum_{n\in L}-\log p_\theta(y_n\mid\mathbf x_n)\) (plus regularization).
  2. For each \(\mathbf x_m\in\mathcal D_U\), the pseudo-label is \(\hat y_m=\arg\max_cp_{\theta_t}(y=c\mid\mathbf x_m)\) and the confidence is \(s_m=\max_cp_{\theta_t}(y=c\mid\mathbf x_m)\).
  3. Selection metric: keep \(S_t=\{m: s_m\ge\tau\}\) [Yar95; RHS05]. With two classes, \(s_m\ge0.5\) always holds, so τ = 0.5 keeps everything.
  4. Refit: \(\theta_{t+1}=\arg\min_\theta\sum_{n\in L}-\log p_\theta(y_n\mid\mathbf x_n)+\sum_{m\in S_t}-\log p_\theta(\hat y_m\mid\mathbf x_m)\).
  5. Repeat until \(S_t\) and the \(\hat y_m\) stop changing. If \(\theta_{t+1}\) predicts every \(\hat y_m\) it was trained on, nothing changes: a fixed point, right or wrong.
The online variant replaces step 4 with one gradient step on a fresh mini-batch. Its unlabeled loss is \(-\log p_\theta(\hat y\mid\mathbf x)=-\max_c\log p_\theta(y=c\mid\mathbf x)\), Eq. 19.16, which leads straight into entropy minimization.

§19.3.2

Entropy minimization: reward confident predictions

Online self-training with cross-entropy minimizes \(\mathcal L=-\max_c\log p_\theta(y=c\mid\mathbf x)\) on unlabeled data (Eq. 19.16). It is smallest when one class gets all the probability, so it quietly pushes the model toward low-entropy, high-confidence predictions. Entropy minimization [GB05] does that on purpose:

\[ \mathcal L=-\sum_{c=1}^Cp_\theta(y=c\mid\mathbf x)\log p_\theta(y=c\mid\mathbf x). \qquad (19.17) \]

Both are cross-entropies \(-\sum_cq_c\log p_c\), with different targets \(q\). Self-training uses the hard one-hot target \(\arg\max p\). Entropy minimization uses the soft target \(p\) itself. Sharpening with a temperature \(T\), \(q_c\propto p_c^{1/T}\), moves between them (MixMatch). \(T=1\) gives entropy minimization, and \(T\to0\) gives hard self-training.

Explain itAt your level

This rule tells the model: "Don't be wishy-washy."

Suppose the model looks at a picture and says "50% cat, 50% dog." That's a shrug. Entropy is a number that measures how much of a shrug an answer is. A 50/50 answer has the most entropy, and a 100/0 answer has none.

Entropy minimization gives the model a small penalty every time it shrugs at an unlabeled picture. Self-training is the strict version: "Pick your favorite answer and act as if you're totally sure." The sharpening knob \(T\) lets you choose anything in between.

For two classes with probability \(p\) for class B, the entropy is \(H(p)=-p\ln p-(1-p)\ln(1-p)\). It is 0 when \(p=0\) or \(1\), and largest, \(\ln2\approx0.69\), at \(p=\tfrac12\).

The plot uses the model's raw score (logit) \(z\) on the horizontal axis, with \(p=\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})\). All three losses peak at \(z=0\), where the model is least sure, and drop as \(|z|\) grows. Self-training's curve has a sharp corner at 0. Entropy is smooth and flat on top. Sharpening with \(T=\tfrac12\) falls in between.

Drag across the plot to move \(z\), and change \(T\) to see the target \(q\) move from "soft" (\(q=p\)) toward "hard" (one-hot).

Write every loss as a cross-entropy \(H(q,p)=-\sum_cq_c\log p_c\):

  • Self-training (19.16): \(q=\text{onehot}(\arg\max_cp_c)\), so \(H(q,p)=-\log\max_cp_c\).
  • Entropy minimization (19.17): \(q=p\), so \(H(p,p)=H(p)\).
  • Sharpened: \(q_c=p_c^{1/T}/\sum_kp_k^{1/T}\). For two classes, \(q=\sigma(z/T)\).

All three are minimized by putting all the mass on one class, and all three are \(\ln2\) at \(z=0\). They differ in how hard they push near the boundary and far from it. That changes how fast the model becomes confident, and how much it trusts its early mistakes.

  • Gradients, binary case. \(dH/dz=-z\,p(1-p)\). This is zero at \(z=0\) (an unstable equilibrium) and largest around \(|z|\approx1.5\). Self-training: \(\frac{d}{dz}\operatorname{softplus}(-|z|)=-\operatorname{sgn}(z)\,\sigma(-|z|)\), which has magnitude \(\tfrac12\) right at the boundary. So hard targets push points near the boundary hardest.
  • Stop-gradient matters. MixMatch and UDA treat the sharpened target \(q\) as a constant. The gradient of \(H(q,p)\) with respect to the logits is \(p-q\). With \(T=1\) and a stopped gradient this is exactly zero, so sharpening only does something when \(T<1\). Entropy minimization backpropagates through both copies of \(p\). Same value, different gradient.
  • As a prior. Grandvalet & Bengio (2005) derive the entropy term as a MAP prior that favors small class overlap. MixMatch uses \(T=0.5\). UDA combines sharpening (\(T=0.4\)) with confidence masking.

§19.3.2.1

The cluster assumption: put the boundary where the data isn't

Many SSL methods assume the decision boundary should fall in a low-density region: classes form clusters, and a good boundary separates the clusters instead of cutting through one. Suppose the model is smooth, so its prediction doesn't jump abruptly. Then a boundary through a dense region forces high-entropy predictions on every sample near it. Minimizing average entropy on unlabeled data therefore pushes the boundary out of the dense regions and into the gaps (Fig. 19.10).

Explain itAt your level

If you have to build a fence between two herds of sheep, don't build it through the middle of a herd.

Sheep standing right next to the fence can't tell which side they belong on. They're unsure. If the fence goes through a crowded herd, lots of sheep are unsure. If it goes through the empty field between the herds, almost nobody is near it, so almost nobody is unsure.

Entropy minimization doesn't like unsure sheep, so it keeps sliding the fence until it sits in the empty field. Watch out, though. You could also make nobody unsure by moving the fence far off to the side so that every sheep is in one herd. That's cheating. The few labeled sheep (and a rule from the next section) stop it.

A smooth classifier goes from "surely A" to "surely B" over some distance. Points inside that strip get answers near 50/50, which means high entropy. So average entropy over unlabeled points ≈ how many points sit near the boundary.

The right-hand plot slides the boundary sideways (keeping its angle) and shows the average entropy at each position. It has a valley in the gap between the clusters. That valley is where entropy minimization pulls the boundary.

The curve also drops at both ends, where the boundary has left the data entirely and every point is called the same class. Draw B starts close enough to that trap that plain entropy minimization falls into it. Turn on the balance term (§19.3.2.2) and run again.

Objective in the demo, with a linear logistic model \(p(B\mid\mathbf x)=\sigma(\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x+b)\):

\[ \mathcal J=\tfrac1{N_L}\textstyle\sum_{L}\text{CE}+\lambda_u\,\tfrac1{N_U}\sum_UH(p_m)-\lambda_b\,H(\bar p)+\tfrac{\kappa}{2}\|\mathbf w\|^2, \]

where \(\bar p=\frac1{N_U}\sum_mp_m\). With \(\lambda_b=0\) this is entropy minimization. With \(\lambda_u=\lambda_b\) it is the mutual-information loss of §19.3.2.2.

Entropy minimization also likes a large \(\|\mathbf w\|\), because scaling up the logits makes every prediction more confident. The small weight decay \(\kappa\) keeps that in check. Watch \(\|\mathbf w\|\) grow while the boundary rotates into the gap.

  • Low-density separation. The transductive SVM (Vapnik; Joachims 1999) maximizes the margin over labeled and unlabeled points. Its hinge on unlabeled points, \(\max(0,1-|f(\mathbf x)|)\), is a non-smooth cousin of the entropy penalty. Chapelle & Zien (2005) make the link explicit. Both objectives are non-convex, so the starting point matters (Draw A vs B).
  • Balance constraints. TSVMs need a constraint that the fraction of unlabeled points given each class matches the labeled data. Without it, they put all unlabeled points in one class. That is the same degenerate solution as here, and the \(H(\bar p)\) term plays the same role.
  • Smoothness isn't automatic for deep nets. The argument needs \(f\) to change slowly in input space. A deep net can make a sharp, confident boundary through a cluster. That is why entropy minimization is usually paired with consistency or adversarial smoothing (VAT, Miyato et al. 2018) rather than used alone.

Math, slowlyWhy entropy pushes the boundary into gaps

Mean entropy counts the points near the boundary

  1. Linear model: \(z=\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x+b\), \(p=\sigma(z)\). The signed distance to the boundary is \(d=z/\|\mathbf w\|\), so \(z=\|\mathbf w\|d\).
  2. \(H(\sigma(z))\) is about \(\ln2\) for \(|z|\lesssim1\) and falls off like \(|z|e^{-|z|}\) beyond that. So it acts like a bump of width about \(1/\|\mathbf w\|\) around the boundary.
  3. Mean unlabeled entropy \(\approx\int p(\mathbf x)H(\sigma(\|\mathbf w\|d(\mathbf x)))d\mathbf x\approx\) (density of data near the boundary) × (bump area). That is the cluster assumption written as a loss.
  4. Gradient for one unlabeled point: \(\partial H/\partial z=-z\,p(1-p)\). Points on the B side (\(z>0\)) push \(z\) up and points on the A side push it down. Both push the boundary away from themselves.
  5. Degenerate minimum: if \(|z|\) is large for every point (boundary off the data), \(H\approx0\) everywhere. The labeled loss, and the \(-H(\bar p)\) term of §19.3.2.2, rule this out.

§19.3.2.2

Input-output mutual information: confident, and not all one class

Bridle, Heading and MacKay [BHM92] get entropy minimization from a different starting point: maximize the mutual information between the input and the predicted label. Negated, it splits into two terms:

\[ \mathcal L(y;\mathbf x)=-\mathbb E_{\mathbf x}\Big[\sum_ip(y_i\mid\mathbf x)\log p(y_i\mid\mathbf x)\Big]+\sum_i\mathbb E_{\mathbf x}[p(y_i\mid\mathbf x)]\log\mathbb E_{\mathbf x}[p(y_i\mid\mathbf x)]. \qquad (19.25) \]

The first term is the mean entropy, which makes each prediction confident. The second term equals \(-H(\bar p)\), and minimizing it makes the average prediction spread out.

So the first term is entropy minimization. The second wants the average prediction \(\bar p=\mathbb E_{\mathbf x}[p(y\mid\mathbf x)]\) to have high entropy, so every class gets used. That is only appropriate when we know a priori that the classes are about equally likely.

Explain itAt your level

Two rules: be sure about each one, and don't put everyone in the same box.

Entropy minimization alone has a cheat. If the model says "box 1, totally sure!" for every single thing, it's never unsure, and it gets a perfect score. But that's useless.

Mutual information adds a second rule: if you look at all the answers together, every box should get used. Now the only way to win is to be sure about each thing and spread things across the boxes.

Nine unlabeled points, three classes. Each point gets a bar chart of probabilities. Two numbers:

  • Average of the entropies, \(\mathbb E[H(p)]\): how unsure the model is about a typical point. We want this low.
  • Entropy of the average, \(H(\bar p)\): average the nine bar charts into one, then measure how spread out that is. We want this high (at most \(\ln3\approx1.10\)).

Mutual information is \(I=H(\bar p)-\mathbb E[H(p)]\). Turn confidence up and the pull toward class A all the way up. The entropy-minimization loss is perfect, near 0, but \(I\approx0\) too. Now turn the pull back to 0 and \(I\) reaches its maximum, \(\ln3\).

Equations 19.18–19.25 are the identity \(I(y;\mathbf x)=H(y)-H(y\mid\mathbf x)\) written out for a classifier:

  • \(H(y\mid\mathbf x)=\mathbb E_{\mathbf x}[H(p(y\mid\mathbf x))]\): mean per-point entropy, the entropy-minimization term.
  • \(H(y)=H(\bar p)\) with \(p(y)=\int p(\mathbf x)p(y\mid\mathbf x)d\mathbf x=\mathbb E_{\mathbf x}[p(y\mid\mathbf x)]\): the marginal over predicted labels.

Minimizing \(-I\) gives confident and balanced predictions. In practice the expectation is a mean over the unlabeled set or a mini-batch. To match a known non-uniform class prior \(\pi\), replace \(-H(\bar p)\) with \(\mathrm{KL}(\bar p\,\|\,\pi)\).

  • Lineage. Bridle, Heading & MacKay (1992), "Unsupervised classifiers, mutual information and phantom targets." Krause, Perona & Gomes (2010), Regularized Information Maximization (RIM), add a weight penalty and use it for discriminative clustering. IIC (Ji et al. 2019) maximizes MI between the predictions for two augmentations of the same image.
  • Mini-batch bias. \(H(\bar p)\) is a nonlinear function of a mean, so estimating it on a batch is biased, and small batches can't represent many classes. Implementations use large batches or a running average of \(\bar p\).
  • Relation to TSVM balancing. \(H(\bar p)\) is a soft version of the class-proportion constraint in transductive SVMs, and of the "distribution alignment" step in ReMixMatch, which rescales predictions so \(\bar p\) matches the labeled class marginal.

From mutual information to a loss

  1. \(I(y;\mathbf x)=\iint p(y,\mathbf x)\log\dfrac{p(y,\mathbf x)}{p(y)p(\mathbf x)}\,dy\,d\mathbf x\). (19.18) Definition, §6.3.
  2. Write \(p(y,\mathbf x)=p(y\mid\mathbf x)p(\mathbf x)\) and cancel \(p(\mathbf x)\) inside the log: \(=\int p(\mathbf x)\int p(y\mid\mathbf x)\log\dfrac{p(y\mid\mathbf x)}{p(y)}\,dy\,d\mathbf x\). (19.19–19.20)
  3. The marginal is \(p(y)=\int p(\mathbf x)p(y\mid\mathbf x)d\mathbf x=\mathbb E_{\mathbf x}[p(y\mid\mathbf x)]\). (19.21)
  4. Outer integral → \(\mathbb E_{\mathbf x}\), inner integral → sum over the \(L\) classes: \(I=\mathbb E_{\mathbf x}\Big[\sum_ip(y_i\mid\mathbf x)\log\dfrac{p(y_i\mid\mathbf x)}{\mathbb E_{\mathbf x}[p(y_i\mid\mathbf x)]}\Big]\). (19.22)
  5. Split the log. The second piece is \(\sum_i\mathbb E_{\mathbf x}[p(y_i\mid\mathbf x)]\log\mathbb E_{\mathbf x}[p(y_i\mid\mathbf x)]=\sum_i\bar p_i\log\bar p_i=-H(\bar p)\). (19.23–19.24)
  6. So \(I=H(\bar p)-\mathbb E_{\mathbf x}[H(p(y\mid\mathbf x))]\), and the loss is \(\mathcal L=-I=\mathbb E_{\mathbf x}[H(p)]-H(\bar p)\). (19.25) Range: \(-\log L\le\mathcal L\le0\).
In (19.24) the book writes the second term as \(\sum_i\mathbb E_{\mathbf x}[p(y_i\mid\mathbf x)\log\mathbb E_{\mathbf x}[p(y_i\mid\mathbf x)]]\). The inner \(\log\mathbb E_{\mathbf x}[\cdot]\) is a constant with respect to the outer expectation, so this equals \(\sum_i\bar p_i\log\bar p_i\).

§19.3.3

Co-training and tri-training: models that label data for each other

Co-training [BM98] assumes two complementary views of each example, two sets of features that are each good enough to train a reasonable classifier. Train one model per view. When one model is confident about an unlabeled point (low entropy) and the other is not (high entropy), add that point, with the confident model's label, to the unsure model's training set. Retrain and repeat.

Two independent views are a strong assumption. Tri-training [ZL05] needs no views. It trains three models on bootstrap samples of the labeled data, and when two of them agree on an unlabeled point, that label goes to the third.

Explain itAt your level

Two friends identify animals at the zoo. One can only see them, and the other can only hear them.

Sometimes the "eyes" friend is sure ("That's a lion, I can see the mane!") but the "ears" friend isn't sure, because the lion is quiet. So the eyes friend tells the ears friend: "That one is a lion. Remember what it sounded like." Other times, the ears friend is sure because of the roar, and teaches the eyes friend.

This works because seeing and hearing are different clues. If both friends could only see, they'd be confused by the same animals and couldn't help each other.

Tri-training uses three friends. When two of them agree, they teach the third.

Blum and Mitchell's example: classify web pages as "course homepage" or not. View 1 is the words on the page. View 2 is the words in links pointing to the page, like "my CS 473 page." Either view alone works fairly well.

The trick: a page that is hard for view 1 is usually not especially hard for view 2. So view 2's confident answers teach view 1 about exactly the cases it struggles with, and vice versa.

In the Play box both plots show the same 300 points, each in a different view. Hover a point to find it in the other plot. Switch to correlated views, where view 2 is just a rotated copy of view 1. Now the hard points are hard for both models, and co-training stalls.

Co-training loop: fit \(f_1\) on view 1 and \(f_2\) on view 2 of \(\mathcal D_L\). For each unlabeled \(\mathbf x\), if \(f_1\) is confident (\(\max p\ge\tau\)) and \(f_2\) isn't, add \((\mathbf x,\hat y_1)\) to \(f_2\)'s training set, and symmetrically. Retrain. Assumptions: each view is sufficient, and the views are conditionally independent given \(y\).

Tri-training: bootstrap \(\mathcal D_L\) three times (sampling with replacement), giving three somewhat different models. For each \(\mathbf x\in\mathcal D_U\) and each model \(k\), if the other two agree on \(\hat y\), add \((\mathbf x,\hat y)\) to model \(k\)'s set. Retrain on labeled + new pseudo-labels and repeat. "Two different models agree" is the selection metric.

  • PAC guarantee. Blum & Mitchell (1998) show that if the views are conditionally independent given \(y\) and the target is learnable from each view, a weakly useful initial predictor can be boosted to arbitrary accuracy using only unlabeled data. The reason: conditional independence makes a confident view-1 label behave like a random labeled example for view 2, with class-dependent noise but no selection bias.
  • Weaker conditions. Balcan, Blum & Yang (2005) replace independence with "ε-expansion": the set of confident examples must keep growing into the region where the other view is uncertain. Co-regularization (Sindhwani et al. 2005) turns view agreement into a loss term.
  • Tri-training details. Zhou & Li's (2005) original algorithm only adds the agreed labels when an error-rate condition (estimated on labeled data) guarantees that the extra examples outweigh their noise. The book's simplified version, used here, can let all three models converge to the same answer, after which nobody can teach anybody.

Math, slowlyWhy conditional independence matters

Co-training's assumption, in one line

  1. Each example is \(\mathbf x=(\mathbf x^{(1)},\mathbf x^{(2)})\), and each view is sufficient: there are \(f_1,f_2\) with \(f_1(\mathbf x^{(1)})=f_2(\mathbf x^{(2)})=y\) (approximately).
  2. Conditional independence: \(p(\mathbf x^{(1)},\mathbf x^{(2)}\mid y)=p(\mathbf x^{(1)}\mid y)\,p(\mathbf x^{(2)}\mid y)\).
  3. Model 1 selects points by looking at \(\mathbf x^{(1)}\) only. Under (2), given the class, their \(\mathbf x^{(2)}\) values are still a random draw from \(p(\mathbf x^{(2)}\mid y)\). Model 2 receives fresh, unbiased examples, including ones that are hard in view 2.
  4. If instead \(\mathbf x^{(2)}\approx g(\mathbf x^{(1)})\), the points model 1 is sure about are the ones model 2 is also sure about. The "confident in one, unsure in the other" set is tiny and full of mistakes.
  5. Combining at test time: with independent views, \(\log\frac{p(B\mid\mathbf x)}{p(A\mid\mathbf x)}\approx\log\frac{p(B\mid\mathbf x^{(1)})}{p(A\mid\mathbf x^{(1)})}+\log\frac{p(B\mid\mathbf x^{(2)})}{p(A\mid\mathbf x^{(2)})}-\log\frac{p(B)}{p(A)}\). Naive Bayes over views. The demo has balanced classes, so the last term is 0.

Tri-training as a selection metric

  1. Bootstrap: \(\mathcal D_L^{(k)}\) = \(N_L\) draws with replacement from \(\mathcal D_L\), for \(k=1,2,3\). Fit \(f_k\) on each.
  2. For each \(k\) and \(\mathbf x\in\mathcal D_U\): if \(f_i(\mathbf x)=f_j(\mathbf x)\) for the other two models \(i,j\), add \((\mathbf x,f_i(\mathbf x))\) to \(S_k\).
  3. Refit \(f_k\) on \(\mathcal D_L^{(k)}\cup S_k\), and repeat. If \(f_i\) and \(f_j\) make independent errors at rate \(e\), then wrong agreements occur at rate about \(e^2\), which is why agreement is a decent filter. Their errors become correlated as the models converge.

§19.3.4

Label propagation on graphs: let labels flow to similar points

The manifold assumption: if two points are similar, they probably share a label. Build a graph whose nodes are all \(M\) labeled and \(N\) unlabeled examples, with edge weights \(w_{ij}\ge0\) measuring similarity. Then let the known labels propagate along the edges until neighboring nodes disagree as little as possible [ZG02].

\[ T_{ij}=\frac{w_{ij}}{\sum_kw_{kj}}\quad(19.26),\qquad \text{repeat:}\quad \mathbf Y\leftarrow\mathbf T\mathbf Y,\quad Y_{ic}\leftarrow\frac{Y_{ic}}{\sum_kY_{ik}},\quad \text{reset labeled rows to one-hot.} \]

Here \(\mathbf Y\) is the \((M+N)\times C\) matrix of class distributions. This converges to a single fixed point, and finding it directly costs one inversion of the unlabeled-to-unlabeled transition matrix. Label propagation is transductive: it labels this particular \(\mathcal D_U\). Train any classifier on the result to get an inductive model.

Explain itAt your level

Think of the dots as tied together by strings, short strings between close dots.

Drip red ink on one dot and blue ink on another. The ink seeps along the strings to nearby dots, and it travels best along short, strong strings. Dots you colored on purpose keep their color no matter what.

After a while every dot is mostly red or mostly blue, depending on which ink reached it more easily. Along a curvy path of dots, the ink follows the curve. It doesn't jump across a gap, because there are no strings across the gap.

Each point starts with a "color mix" (how much it thinks it's class A and how much class B). The labeled points are pure colors. Unlabeled ones start at 50/50.

One step: every unlabeled point replaces its mix with a weighted average of its neighbors' mixes, where closer neighbors count more. Labeled points get reset to their true color. Repeat until nothing changes.

The answer depends on which points count as neighbors. Each point connects to its k nearest points, with weight \(e^{-d^2/2\sigma^2}\). Small k follows each moon. On Moons, raise k to about 16 and edges start to cross the gap, so color leaks into the other moon. Then try "noisy moons," where the two classes touch.

The algorithm, as in the book:

  1. \(T_{ij}=w_{ij}/\sum_kw_{kj}\): the probability of propagating node \(j\)'s label to node \(i\).
  2. \(\mathbf Y\leftarrow\mathbf T\mathbf Y\), then renormalize each row to sum to 1.
  3. Clamp: \(Y_{ic}=\mathbb I(y_i=c)\) for labeled \(i\).
  4. Repeat until \(\mathbf Y\) stops changing, then predict \(\hat y_i=\arg\max_cY_{ic}\).

Since rows of \(\mathbf Y\) sum to 1, steps 2–3 equal \(\mathbf Y\leftarrow\bar{\mathbf T}\mathbf Y\) with \(\bar{\mathbf T}\) the row-normalized \(\mathbf T\). Splitting into labeled (L) and unlabeled (U) blocks, the fixed point solves \(\mathbf Y_U=\bar{\mathbf T}_{UU}\mathbf Y_U+\bar{\mathbf T}_{UL}\mathbf Y_L\), so \(\mathbf Y_U=(\mathbf I-\bar{\mathbf T}_{UU})^{-1}\bar{\mathbf T}_{UL}\mathbf Y_L\). The "Jump to fixed point" button computes exactly this.

  • Random-walk view. \(\bar{\mathbf T}\) is row-stochastic. Make labeled nodes absorbing. Then \(Y_{ic}\) at the fixed point is the probability that a walk started at \(i\) is first absorbed at a class-\(c\) labeled node. Uniqueness needs every unlabeled component to reach a labeled node, which makes \(\mathbf I-\bar{\mathbf T}_{UU}\) invertible.
  • Harmonic functions. With the row-normalized \(\mathbf P=\mathbf D^{-1}\mathbf W\) instead, the fixed point is the harmonic solution of Zhu, Ghahramani & Lafferty (2003). It minimizes the Laplacian energy \(\tfrac12\sum_{ij}w_{ij}(f_i-f_j)^2=\mathbf f^\top\mathbf L\mathbf f\) subject to the clamped labels, and each \(f_i\) is the weighted mean of its neighbors. \(\bar{\mathbf T}\) and \(\mathbf P\) agree when all column sums of \(\mathbf W\) are equal.
  • Variants. Zhou et al. (2004), "local and global consistency," use \(\mathbf S=\mathbf D^{-1/2}\mathbf W\mathbf D^{-1/2}\) and soft clamping, \(\mathbf F=(1-\alpha)(\mathbf I-\alpha\mathbf S)^{-1}\mathbf Y\), which tolerates label noise. GCNs (Kipf & Welling 2017) learn features while propagating.
  • Similarity is everything. In high dimensions Euclidean distance is a poor proxy for "same class." Iscen et al. (2019) build the kNN graph on a CNN's embeddings, propagate, train on the result with per-sample certainty weights, and repeat [BRR18; Isc+19].

Math, slowlyEquation 19.26 and the fixed point

From the iteration to one linear solve

  1. Weights: here \(w_{ij}=\exp(-\|\mathbf x_i-\mathbf x_j\|^2/2\sigma^2)\) if \(j\) is among \(i\)'s \(k\) nearest neighbors or vice versa, and 0 otherwise. Any non-negative similarity works [Zhu05, ch. 3].
  2. \(T_{ij}=w_{ij}/\sum_kw_{kj}\): column \(j\) of \(\mathbf T\) sums to 1, so it splits node \(j\)'s label among its neighbors. (19.26)
  3. Propagate: \((\mathbf T\mathbf Y)_i=\sum_jT_{ij}\mathbf Y_j\). If every row of \(\mathbf Y\) sums to 1, this row sums to \(\sum_jT_{ij}\), and renormalizing gives \(\sum_j\bar T_{ij}\mathbf Y_j\) with \(\bar T_{ij}=T_{ij}/\sum_lT_{il}\). So the renormalization step just uses the row-normalized \(\bar{\mathbf T}\).
  4. Clamp the labeled rows. In block form: \(\mathbf Y_U\leftarrow\bar{\mathbf T}_{UU}\mathbf Y_U+\bar{\mathbf T}_{UL}\mathbf Y_L\).
  5. Fixed point: \(\mathbf Y_U=(\mathbf I-\bar{\mathbf T}_{UU})^{-1}\bar{\mathbf T}_{UL}\mathbf Y_L\). It exists and is unique when every unlabeled node has a path to a labeled one. Then \(\bar{\mathbf T}_{UU}\) has spectral radius below 1 and the iteration converges geometrically at that rate. That is why far-away nodes take hundreds of steps.
  6. Cost: solving an \(N\times N\) system is \(O(N^3)\) if dense, cheaper with sparse kNN graphs and iterative solvers. Predict \(\hat y_i=\arg\max_cY_{ic}\).
Transductive: you get labels only for the nodes in the graph. A new test point needs either a new graph or an inductive classifier trained on the propagated labels. The "inductive" checkbox fits RBF logistic regression to the soft labels \(Y_{iB}\).

§19.3.5

Consistency regularization: a small nudge shouldn't change the answer

Perturbing an input (or the model) a little shouldn't change the model's output much. Checking that needs only the model's outputs and no labels, so it works on unlabeled data. This idea began as "learning with pseudo-ensembles" [BAP14], soon followed by [LA16; SJT16]. Sample a perturbed copy \(\mathbf x'\sim q(\mathbf x'\mid\mathbf x)\), for example with augmentation, noise or dropout, and penalize any change in the prediction:

\[ \mathcal L(\theta)=-\sum_{i=1}^M\log p_\theta(y=y_i\mid\mathbf x_i)+\lambda\sum_{j=1}^N\big\|p_\theta(y\mid\mathbf x_j)-p_\theta(y\mid\mathbf x_j')\big\|^2. \qquad (19.27) \]

The clean prediction \(p_\theta(y\mid\mathbf x_j)\) is usually treated as a fixed target, with no gradient through it. There are three main design choices. The first is \(\lambda\): too large and the model reinforces its own bad early predictions, so it is usually ramped up from 0. The second is \(q(\mathbf x'\mid\mathbf x)\): it must not change the label. Strong augmentations work well, and virtual adversarial training (VAT) picks the perturbation that changes the output most. The third is the distance: squared error (the Brier score) or KL divergence.

Explain itAt your level

A cat is still a cat if you tilt the picture a little.

We don't know the right answer for most pictures. But we do know one rule: if you wiggle a picture slightly (tilt it, make it a bit blurry), the answer shouldn't change. So we show the model a picture and a wiggled copy, and give it a penalty if it gives two different answers.

Where are answers likely to flip when you wiggle? Right next to the line between "cat" and "dog." So following this rule pushes that line away from the pictures, into empty space. That's the same "put the fence in the empty field" idea from before.

Two warnings. Don't wiggle so hard that the cat turns into something else. And at the start, when the model knows almost nothing, don't make this rule too important yet.

For each unlabeled point \(x\), make a nearby copy \(x'\). In the demo that means adding a little random noise. Ask the model for its probabilities at both points, and add the squared difference to the loss. Labeled points still use the normal loss.

A smooth model only changes its answer quickly near its decision boundary. So "keep answers the same when I jiggle the data points" really means "keep the boundary away from the data points." On the two moons it slides the boundary into the gap, even for Draw B, where self-training failed.

Try a jiggle size σ that is too big, 0.6 or more. Now the copies of a point land in the other moon, so the label really does change, and the method falls apart. Good augmentations keep the label the same.

  • Stochasticity everywhere. \(q(\mathbf x'\mid\mathbf x)\) can be augmentation (rotation, noise, crops), and the model itself can be stochastic (dropout, weight noise [Gra11]). The demo uses Gaussian input noise or VAT.
  • Stop-gradient. Only \(p_\theta(y\mid\mathbf x')\) is moved toward \(p_\theta(y\mid\mathbf x)\). With binary outputs, the gradient of \(2(p-q')^2\) with respect to the perturbed logit \(z'\) is \(4(q'-p)\,q'(1-q')\). For \(\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q')\) it is \(q'-p\).
  • λ ramp-up. Early on the model is poor, and a large \(\lambda\) would make it match its own bad predictions (like confirmation bias). A common schedule is \(\lambda(t)=\lambda\,e^{-5(1-t/T)^2}\) for \(t\le T\) [LA16].
  • VAT [Miy+18]: \(\boldsymbol\delta\approx\arg\max_{\boldsymbol\delta}\mathrm{KL}(p_\theta(y\mid\mathbf x)\,\|\,p_\theta(y\mid\mathbf x+\boldsymbol\delta))\). Sample \(\mathbf d\sim\mathcal N(0,\mathbf I)\), set \(\boldsymbol\delta\leftarrow\nabla_{\boldsymbol\delta}\mathrm{KL}\big|_{\boldsymbol\delta=\xi\mathbf d}\) (19.28), then use \(\mathbf x'=\mathbf x+\epsilon\,\boldsymbol\delta/\|\boldsymbol\delta\|_2\) (19.29). No domain knowledge is needed, only a step size \(\epsilon\).
  • Squared error vs KL (Fig. 19.11): squared error saturates (gradient → 0) when the two predictions disagree confidently. KL grows without bound and has the same scale as the labeled cross-entropy, which makes \(\lambda\) easier to tune.
  • Family tree. The Π-model and temporal ensembling [LA16] use two stochastic passes, or a running average of past predictions, as the target. Mean Teacher [TV17] uses a weight-EMA teacher. VAT [Miy+18] uses adversarial perturbations. UDA [Xie+19] uses RandAugment or back-translation with KL and confidence masking. MixMatch/ReMixMatch [Ber+19b; Ber+19a] use sharpened, averaged targets plus mixup. FixMatch [Soh+20] uses a hard pseudo-label from a weak view as the target for a strong view.
  • Why it works. For a small isotropic perturbation, \(\mathbb E\|p(\mathbf x)-p(\mathbf x+\boldsymbol\eta)\|^2\approx\sigma^2\|\nabla_{\mathbf x}p(\mathbf x)\|_F^2\). So the loss penalizes the input-gradient at data points: a data-dependent smoothness (Jacobian) penalty that pushes the boundary into low-density regions. That is the cluster assumption without entropy's degenerate "all one class" solution. VAT penalizes the top eigenvalue of the KL's input Hessian instead of its trace.
  • Binary VAT. For a sigmoid output the KL Hessian in input space is \(q(1-q)\nabla_{\mathbf x}z\nabla_{\mathbf x}z^\top\), which has rank 1. One power-iteration step from any \(\mathbf d\) not orthogonal to \(\nabla_{\mathbf x}z\) gives \(\boldsymbol\delta\propto\pm\nabla_{\mathbf x}z\): straight across the boundary. That is what the arrows in the demo show.
  • Optimization geometry. With consistency losses, SGD keeps moving far in weight space late in training. Athiwaratkun et al. [Ath+19] exploit this with a variant of stochastic weight averaging (fast-SWA) and get state-of-the-art SSL results.

The loss and its gradient

  1. Perturb: \(\mathbf x_j'\sim q(\mathbf x'\mid\mathbf x_j)\), a fresh sample at every step.
  2. Loss: \(-\sum_i\log p_\theta(y_i\mid\mathbf x_i)+\lambda\sum_j\|p_\theta(y\mid\mathbf x_j)-p_\theta(y\mid\mathbf x_j')\|^2\). (19.27)
  3. Stop-gradient: treat \(p_j=p_\theta(y\mid\mathbf x_j)\) as a constant. Binary, with \(q_j'=\sigma(z_j')\): \(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial z_j'}2(p_j-q_j')^2=4(q_j'-p_j)\,q_j'(1-q_j')\). The factor \(q'(1-q')\) is why squared error saturates.
  4. KL instead: \(\mathrm{KL}(p_j\,\|\,q_j')=p_j\log\frac{p_j}{q_j'}+(1-p_j)\log\frac{1-p_j}{1-q_j'}\), with \(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial z_j'}=q_j'-p_j\), the same form as the labeled cross-entropy gradient.
  5. Small noise: \(\mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\eta\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2\mathbf I)}\|p(\mathbf x)-p(\mathbf x+\boldsymbol\eta)\|^2\approx\sigma^2\|\nabla_{\mathbf x}p(\mathbf x)\|^2\): a smoothness penalty at the data.

Virtual adversarial training

  1. Target: \(\boldsymbol\delta^*=\arg\max_{\|\boldsymbol\delta\|\le\epsilon}\mathrm{KL}\big(p_\theta(y\mid\mathbf x)\,\|\,p_\theta(y\mid\mathbf x+\boldsymbol\delta)\big)\).
  2. At \(\boldsymbol\delta=0\) the KL and its gradient are both 0, so the maximizer is the top eigenvector of the Hessian \(\mathbf H\). One power-iteration step: \(\mathbf d\sim\mathcal N(0,\mathbf I)\), \(\boldsymbol\delta\leftarrow\nabla_{\boldsymbol\delta}\mathrm{KL}\big|_{\boldsymbol\delta=\xi\mathbf d}\approx\xi\mathbf H\mathbf d\), with \(\xi\approx10^{-6}\). (19.28)
  3. Rescale: \(\mathbf x'=\mathbf x+\epsilon\,\boldsymbol\delta/\|\boldsymbol\delta\|_2\). (19.29) Then apply (19.27) as usual.
  4. Binary sigmoid model: \(\nabla_{\boldsymbol\delta}\mathrm{KL}=(q-p)\nabla_{\mathbf x}z(\mathbf x+\boldsymbol\delta)\), so \(\boldsymbol\delta\) points along \(\pm\nabla_{\mathbf x}z\): straight toward or away from the boundary. The demo computes this exactly, with \(\xi=10^{-6}\).
For the demo's RBF features \(\phi_k(\mathbf x)=e^{-g\|\mathbf x-\mathbf c_k\|^2}\), the input gradient is \(\nabla_{\mathbf x}z=\sum_kw_k\phi_k(\mathbf x)\cdot(-2g)(\mathbf x-\mathbf c_k)\).
